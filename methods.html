What the tool does

You give a load in MW and a county. The tool converts the load to annual electricity, assigns that electricity to power plants, and reports three things: CO2, the fine-particle (PM2.5) increment downwind with the deaths attributable to it, and water consumed on site and at the plants.

Electricity

Annual energy = MW × PUE (if the MW figure is IT or process load) × load factor × 8,760 h. The load factor is the average draw as a share of the MW entered. The 0.70 default for data centers is a starting value, not a measurement: Berkeley Lab researchers have described working with about 70% average server utilisation and a 30-80% uncertainty range, and server utilisation is not the same thing as a facility's load factor. Grid options divide by (1 − transmission and distribution loss) to get generation. The default loss is EPA eGRID2023's grid gross loss for the interconnection (4.2% Eastern, 4.1% Western, 4.2% ERCOT). The carbon-free share is a user what-if with no data behind it and defaults to zero.

Which plants
  • Average of all plants: every plant in the county's eGRID subregion, in proportion to its 2023 net generation (EPA eGRID2023).
  • Plants that respond to new load (non-baseload): combustion plants in the subregion weighted by eGRID's non-baseload factor, EPA's proxy for the plants that respond when load changes. It is not a dispatch model and does not capture new capacity built to serve the load.
  • A mix you set yourself: fuel shares chosen by the user, starting from the subregion's current shares by each plant's primary fuel. Each fuel is represented by the subregion's existing plants of that fuel, weighted by generation, so the result shows a different balance of today's plants, not new plants in new places. Fuels supplying under 0.5% of the subregion's electricity are held fixed, because a handful of rarely-run units gives unstable per-MWh rates.
  • On-site gas: emission rates you enter, released in the host county.
  • Carbon-free power matched every hour: no operational emissions. The slider on the grid options applies the same idea to part of the load.
Air quality and health

Plant SO2, NOx and primary PM2.5 are run through the InMAP Source-Receptor Matrix (ISRM v1.2.1; Goodkind et al. 2019, PNAS 116:8775), which gives the annual-average PM2.5 change in 52,411 receptor cells per unit of emissions in each source cell. Pathways used: primary PM2.5, sulfate from SO2, nitrate from NOx. Each plant's emissions are split between the two ISRM release layers that bracket an assumed effective release height by fuel. Cells are aggregated to counties by overlapping area. The map shows counties when zoomed out and the model's own cells (1 km in dense cities, up to 48 km in rural areas) when zoomed in; that grid is the finest scale the model supports.

Deaths = β × ΔPM2.5 × population × baseline all-cause mortality, summed over cells, with the population and mortality fields carried in the ISRM file. The relation is linear here because the increments are small. Three concentration-response functions are offered: Krewski et al. (2009), RR 1.06 per 10 µg/m3 (default); Lepeule et al. (2012), RR 1.14; and the Global Exposure Mortality Model (GEMM; Burnett et al. 2018, non-communicable disease and lower respiratory infection deaths at ages 25+), used through its slope at 8 µg/m3, roughly the US population-weighted mean, and scaled by 0.87, the approximate share of US deaths from those causes at those ages. That gives about RR 1.09 per 10 µg/m3, 1.5 times Krewski. GEMM is non-linear: its slope is 1.9 times Krewski at 6 µg/m3 and 1.3 times at 10, so in cleaner air the single slope understates it.

Backup diesel generators

Optional, and added to whichever supply option is chosen. Annual generator output = number of units × kW × hours run per year × average load while running. Emission rates are EPA limits for engines of 560 kW and over. Tier 2 (40 CFR 89.112): 6.4 g/kWh NMHC+NOx, all counted as NOx (an upper bound), and 0.20 g/kWh PM, all counted as PM2.5. Tier 4 final generator sets (40 CFR 1039.101): 0.67 g/kWh NOx and 0.03 g/kWh PM. Engines used only for emergencies are exempt from Tier 4, so most backup units are Tier 2. SO2 assumes ultra-low-sulfur diesel (15 ppm) with the AP-42 section 3.4 factor (0.007 g/kWh); CO2 is about 700 g/kWh (AP-42 3.4 average fuel use, 7,000 Btu/hp-hr, × 73.96 kg CO2/MMBtu for distillate fuel). Federal rules let emergency engines run up to 100 hours a year for maintenance, testing and limited non-emergency use (40 CFR 60.4211(f)); real outages are not capped. The default count is enough 2.5 MW units to carry the facility at full load, plus one spare. Emissions are released in the site's grid cell at the height entered (10 m by default, which the model treats as ground level) and go through the same source-receptor matrix as on-site power. The grid electricity the generators displace while running is not subtracted.

Who is exposed

Exposure by group is computed at the census block group, the smallest unit with American Community Survey income and poverty data. Each of about 238,000 contiguous-US block groups (2023 boundaries) takes the area-weighted average of the model grid cells it overlaps; cells are 1 km in cities and up to 48 km in remote areas, so in sparsely populated places neighbouring block groups share a value. People are counted from the ACS 2019–2023 five-year estimates: race and ethnicity (B03002), poverty ratio (C17002), educational attainment for adults 25 and over (B15003), and block-group median household income (B19013). Income fifths rank block groups by median household income, with cut points set so that each fifth holds a fifth of the people; about 5% of people live in block groups without a published median and are left out of the income fifths only. A group's exposure is the population-weighted mean PM2.5 increment of its members; deaths use the model's baseline mortality rate in each cell, so they differ from the headline figure, which uses the model's own (around 2010) population, by about the population growth since then (roughly 6%). For on-site power and backup generators, the same groups are computed for a source in the site’s own grid cell (one pass of the matrix for every cell, stored in data/onsite_groups.rds). Assigning every person in a block group its median income, and the block group's area-average concentration, are the main simplifications.

Noise

A screening estimate, not a noise study. Generators: the makers' published A-weighted level at 7 m, at full load, for a 2,500 kW set (Caterpillar 3516C with a Level 2 enclosure, 74 dB(A); with a Level 1 enclosure, 79; MTU 20V4000 open set, 97.5, exhaust excluded), converted to sound power (+24.9 dB) and scaled by rating. A routine test runs the number of engines you set; an outage runs every engine. Cooling: air-cooled chillers at 92, 95 or 98 dB(A) of sound power per MW of heat rejected (extra-low-noise, low-noise and standard units; Trane RTAF published data), with heat rejected equal to the facility's full load. Sound spreads by the simplified A-weighted method of ISO 9613-2: downwind, flat ground, no barriers, sources at a point (3 m for generators, 15 m for cooling), receivers at 1.5 m. People within each level use the population density of the model grid cell around the site. Thresholds: 55 dB(A), Loudoun County's residential limit; 45 dB(A), about 10 dB above a quiet suburban night. Buildings and walls often cut 5 to 15 dB; A-weighting understates the low-frequency hum residents report most. The Virginia data centers page applies the same method to every permitted site, with people counted on a 50 m grid from block groups (analysis/06_va_noise.R; assumptions in analysis/06_va_noise_assumptions.md).

Virginia data centers

Virginia DEQ's air permits for data center generators (194 listed; 193 saved by hand and read; 191 current). Annual limits, engines, ratings, testing hours and operating terms are read from each permit (scripts/08, with hand-read values for permits that could not be read reliably), sites are geocoded where the address can be matched (scripts/09; otherwise the county's centre), and each site's emissions are run through the same source-receptor matrix as the rest of the tool under three cases: routine testing (testing hours at 75% load, EPA Tier 2 rates), testing plus 50 hours of grid support (SCR credited in those hours), and the permitted maximum (analysis/05_va_permits.R).

Water

On site: litres per kWh of IT or process energy, as entered. At power plants: plant-level cooling water consumption and withdrawal reported to EIA (2023) where available, otherwise typical consumption factors by fuel. Hydropower reservoir evaporation is excluded.

Comparison with Chen et al. (2026)

Chen et al. (2026, Environ. Res. Lett. 21 214046) estimate the air quality and health effects of US data center grid electricity in 2023 with an hourly, unit-level dispatch model across 64 power regions, the GEOS-Chem chemical transport model and the GEMM: 513 deaths a year from PM2.5 and 137 from ozone. We ran their state-level demand (152.3 TWh, their Table S1) through this tool (analysis/09_chen2026_comparison.R). With the non-baseload mix the tool gives 71.7 kt NOx and 50.5 kt SO2 against their 61.4 and 39.1 (the average mix gives about half), and 286 PM2.5 deaths with Krewski, about 430 with the GEMM option and 643 with Lepeule. The share of deaths falling in each state agrees closely (correlation 0.88): both put 4–5% of the deaths in Virginia although it has 22% of the load, and Texas, Pennsylvania, Ohio and Illinois near the top. The main differences: this tool puts fewer of the deaths in the Midwest (10% against 15%), as Chen et al. find for the ISRM, whose 2005-era chemistry understates nitrate formation in today's ammonia-rich air; and it puts more in New York and North Carolina, partly because the comparison split each state's load by population. Chen et al. exclude backup generators; the Virginia permits page covers them.

Limitations
  • The ISRM uses 2005 meteorology and background chemistry and census population from around 2010. Nitrate and sulfate formation per tonne has changed as background emissions fell; compared with a full chemistry model for 2023, it understates PM2.5 from NOx in the Midwest (Chen et al. 2026).
  • eGRID has no stack data, so release heights are assumed by fuel category. Height choices move national totals by roughly ten percent in published ISRM comparisons.
  • Plant PM2.5 comes from EPA's draft unit-level file (2021 rates applied to 2023 heat input) or, where missing, from emission factors. See the build report below for coverage.
  • The mixes are annual. Hourly marginal emissions, imports between subregions, and capacity added for the load are not represented. In a dispatch model, Virginia's data center load is met by plants across the PJM grid (Chen et al. 2026); here it is met within its own eGRID subregion.
  • EPA's guidance is to use total-output (average) rates for footprints and inventories and non-baseload rates for rough estimates of emission changes from changes in electricity use.
  • Counties are assigned to the eGRID subregion that contains them; real utility territories overlap. Override the region if you know the serving utility.
  • On-site generation reports totals only. It uses the model grid cell that contains the site when a latitude and longitude are given, and the cell at the county's interior point otherwise.
  • Ozone is not included; for data center grid electricity, Chen et al. (2026) find ozone adds about a quarter to the PM2.5 deaths. NH3 and VOC pathways, construction, and upstream fuel-cycle emissions are not included. Backup generators are included only when switched on, and only at the hours entered; emissions during long outages, and during start-up and low-load testing (when per-kWh emissions are higher), are not represented.
  • Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico are outside the ISRM domain.
